# Seal the core, dispose the cutter: a contained morcellator plan

**Bottom line:** A hand-held, wireless, contained morcellator for Colombia and later the US is realistic, but neither "wireless" nor "disposable" will make it new. LiNA Medical already sells a cleared, fully disposable, battery-powered 15 mm morcellator, and since July 2026 the FDA has a dedicated device type for integrated "power morcellator + containment" systems. The FDA never banned power morcellators. It requires a boxed warning, strict patient selection and use **only with a compatible containment system**. That rule makes the bag part of the morcellator's regulatory identity, and it is where the evidence shows real unmet need: bags tear on trocars and during forced extraction, dye leaks show up in about 9% of "intact" bags, and contained morcellation adds about 40 minutes. The architecture these notes support best is a **sealed, never-sterilized reusable core** (motor, battery, electronics), dropped aseptically into a **single-use sterile shell**. Torque crosses the shell wall through a **face magnetic coupling** across a thin polymer wall, and the core charges **inductively or by battery swap outside the sterile field**. The single-use cutter cartridge comes **pre-joined and interlocked to its own bag port**. That interlock, together with sensing of bag integrity, is the most plausible patentable white space, though no formal search has confirmed it yet. In Colombia the system most likely registers as INVIMA Class IIa. That route uses automatic registration, with IIb as a risk to budget for. The US route is a 510(k) under product code SIM, which requires clinical performance data, or a morcellator-plus-bag split under HET and PMU. The cap of 2,000,000 COP (about **USD 620**) means two very different things. It is generous for the disposable kit (estimated cost USD 30–120) and tight for a reusable handpiece built from premium European parts (estimated USD 330–650). This report is engineering and market research, not legal, regulatory or patent advice.

*Legend used throughout: **[SRC]** = a figure taken from a cited source; **[EST]** = an engineering estimate or inference made in this research, not sourced, to be validated by a bench test or a quote.*

## The FDA restricted morcellation and created two device types instead of banning it

The phrase "FDA banned morcellators" is a myth worth killing early, because it shapes how investors, clinicians and INVIMA reviewers will read the project. The sequence was a safety communication in April 2014, a July 2014 advisory panel, and then an **immediately-in-effect labeling guidance on 25 Nov 2014** that added a boxed warning and contraindications ([FDA guidance](https://fda.gov/media/90012/download); [Federal Register 2014-27857](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2014-11-25/pdf/2014-27857.pdf)). The trigger was FDA's 2014 estimate of occult sarcoma: unsuspected uterine sarcoma in **1 in 352** women operated on for presumed fibroids, and leiomyosarcoma in **1 in 498** ([OBGyn.net](https://europe.obgyn.net/view/commentary-recent-updates-power-morcellator-debate)). Later and larger reviews lowered that risk considerably. AHRQ in 2017 (160 studies, 136,195 women) estimated **1 in 770 to under 1 in 10,000**, and ACOG calls it the most rigorous estimate ([ACOG CO 822](https://www.augs.org/assets/1/6/Uterine_Morcellation_for_Presumed_Leiomyomas.pdf)). Ethicon nonetheless suspended worldwide sales of its Morcellex on 30 April 2014 ([OBG Management](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/Current_labeling_for_laparoscopic_power_morcellators_with_gynecologic_indications.pdf)). This commercial exit, not a regulatory ban, is what most people remember.

The FDA's response was to build a regulatory place for containment. On **7 April 2016** it granted De Novo **DEN150028** to the PneumoLiner, creating 21 CFR 884.4050, "gynecologic laparoscopic power morcellation containment system," Class II with special controls, product code **PMU** ([Federal Register 2016-14627](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2016-06-21/pdf/2016-14627.pdf)). The December 2020 safety communication and final labeling guidance then made containment the norm. Power morcellators should be used "only with a containment system" that is "compatible with the laparoscopic power morcellator," and not in known or suspected malignancy, nor for suspected fibroids in women who are post-menopausal or **over 50**, nor in candidates for en bloc removal ([FDA safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/safety-communications/update-perform-only-contained-morcellation-when-laparoscopic-power-morcellation-appropriate-fda); [FDA guidance](https://fda.gov/media/90012/download)). Two details matter directly for this design. First, the guidance applies "regardless of morcellation mechanism," so going battery-powered or hand-held does not escape the boxed warning. Second, the FDA's 2020 statement that only "one containment system" was authorized is now out of date. PMU has since gained 510(k) clearances for the PneumoLiner (K192898, 2020), the A.M.I. More-Cell-System (K212659, 2021) and the Ark Surgical LapBox (K250212, April 2025) ([Innolitics PMU listing](https://fda.innolitics.com/productcode/PMU)).

The newest and most important change for this project came on **2 July 2026**. The FDA granted De Novo **DEN250034** to Claria Medical (Mountain View, CA) for the Claria System. This created 21 CFR 884.4075, "gynecologic power morcellator and containment system," product code **SIM**, Class II, after which similar devices go through a 510(k) ([FDA DEN250034](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf25/DEN250034.pdf)). Claria's indication is narrow: contained cutting, coring and extraction during laparoscopic hysterectomy, with the container **inserted transvaginally**, limited to premenopausal women under 50 for fibroids (same source). The FDA's morcellator page, updated 20 August 2026, still describes restriction, not prohibition ([FDA LPM page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/surgery-devices/laparoscopic-power-morcellators)). The clinical side has not caught up with regulation. ACOG's Committee Opinion 822 (March 2021) says it is **unknown whether bags prevent spread**, notes that the FDA's position rests on bench and animal testing, and warns that bags can leak, lengthen surgery and block the view of nearby organs ([ACOG CO 822](https://www.augs.org/assets/1/6/Uterine_Morcellation_for_Presumed_Leiomyomas.pdf)). For the father's clinical practice this means patient selection and counseling stay central regardless of the device. For the product it means that leak data collected from the first case onward is both a scientific gap and a commercial asset.

## Three regulatory routes, and the bag decides which one applies

### US: SIM 510(k) versus HET morcellator plus PMU bag

A new hand-held contained morcellator has two realistic FDA routes, and the choice follows almost entirely from whether the bag ships with the device. **Route A, an integrated system**, is now a 510(k) to product code SIM with Claria as predicate. The SIM special controls are the heaviest set in this space. They include **clinical performance testing** that shows deployment, morcellation and removal with containment kept intact and every adverse event evaluated. They also include bench impermeability after morcellation, worst-case morcellation testing, biocompatibility, sterility, shelf life, **EMC, electrical, thermal and mechanical safety**, software verification and hazard analysis, a validated training program and boxed-warning labeling ([DEN250034](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf25/DEN250034.pdf)). The catch is substantial equivalence. A design that enters through the umbilicus or a port, or that targets myomectomy instead of transvaginal hysterectomy, may not be equivalent to Claria and could need its own De Novo [EST, regulatory judgement]. **Route B, a stand-alone morcellator**, is a 510(k) under **HET** (21 CFR 884.1720, "laparoscope, gynecologic and accessories," Class II) ([FDA HET](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpcd/classification.cfm?id=HET)). The Gynecare morcellator was cleared there as K993801 ([Innolitics](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K993801)). The labeling would name a compatible PMU bag, which means compatibility testing against someone else's bag or a separate 510(k) for your own.

The most useful precedent for a cordless design is the **LiNA Xcise 510(k), K101458**. A 15 mm rotating tube with a self-contained motor and battery, single-use and **gamma-sterilized**, it was cleared on bench data alone. The tests covered tube and pull strength, gear life, motor torque, rpm, motor life, **battery and electronics function after gamma sterilization**, heat, vibration, noise, cut rate in g/min and EMC to EN 60601-1-2. **No clinical data was required** ([K101458](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K101458)). That clearance predates the 2020 containment-only policy, so it shows that the electromechanical part is a well-trodden 510(k) problem. The new regulatory burden sits in the bag and in the clinical evidence for the integrated system.

The PMU containment guidance (final, May 2023) shows what bag testing involves. It calls for microbial leakage testing with *B. diminuta* after simulated use, an assay able to detect a single cancer cell crossing the barrier, pull force on aged samples, burst strength with a safety factor over insufflation pressure, puncture testing with worst-case instruments, and permeability testing with a marker no larger than a cancer cell by a method similar to **ASTM F1671** ([FDA guidance PDF](https://fda.gov/media/159294/download)). It sets **no numeric acceptance criteria**. The manufacturer must set and justify them, which is a design-input task in its own right.

### Colombia: likely Class IIa with automatic registration, IIb as the risk case

Under **Decreto 4725 de 2005**, still the governing decree, the classification logic points to Class IIa for both the morcellator and the bag ([Decreto 4725, INVIMA normograma](https://normograma.invima.gov.co/normograma/compilacion/docs/decreto_4725_2005.htm)). Rule 6 places transient-use (under 60 min), surgically invasive devices in IIa. Its exception for reusable surgical instruments (Class I) covers only instruments **not connected to an active device**, so a motorized handpiece does not qualify. Rule 9 puts active therapeutic devices that deliver energy in IIa, unless the energy can be applied "in a potentially hazardous way," which moves them to **IIb** (same source). A kit sold together takes the highest class of its parts [EST, regulatory reading]. The practical difference between the two classes is large. Class I and IIa get **registro sanitario automático**, issued within about two business days of a complete filing and reviewed afterwards. IIb and III require prior evaluation (90 business days by law, as amended by Decreto 582 de 2017) and **clinical studies** (same source). A consultant source puts IIb reviews at 6–8 months in practice and 2026 fees at about **COP 3.9 M per product for I/IIa** and **COP 4.4 M for IIb/III**, with no formal reliance pathway on FDA or CE approvals ([MedDeviceGuide, secondary](https://meddeviceguide.com/blog/colombia-invima-medical-device-registration-guide)). A registration lasts **10 years**. Changes in "design, chemical composition, energy source, manufacturing process" count as significant changes that need a **new registration** ([Decreto 4725](https://normograma.invima.gov.co/normograma/compilacion/docs/decreto_4725_2005.htm)). The motor, battery and blade should therefore be frozen before filing.

Even Class IIa is not light paperwork. Article 18 requires a quality-system certificate (CCAA, BPM or equivalent), technical studies, scientific information and a risk analysis ([Decreto 4725](https://normograma.invima.gov.co/normograma/compilacion/docs/decreto_4725_2005.htm)). Two forward-looking issues remain open. A draft replacement decree published by MinSalud on 23 Dec 2025 would bring in ISO 13485, GMDN and UDI language and take effect 18 months after issue. Whether it was issued in 2026 is unconfirmed ([CERLATAM](https://www.cerlatam.com/normatividad/minsalud-proyecto-de-decreto-23-dic-2025/); [MinSalud draft](https://www.minsalud.gov.co/Normatividad_Nuevo/PD%20Regimen%20Sanitario%20DM.pdf)). No INVIMA safety alert specific to morcellators was found ([INVIMA alerts](https://www.invima.gov.co/alertas-sanitarias/insuflacion-para-laparoscopia-olympus)). Because INVIMA offers no shortcut through foreign approvals, and an INVIMA registration does not substitute for FDA clearance either, the efficient approach is to **build the INVIMA file to FDA and ISO test standards from day one**. That way one set of tests serves both. Colombian clinical use could also feed the SIM clinical-performance requirement if it is run as a properly designed, registered study [EST].

### Standards the test plan will map to

The FDA special controls name the test areas. The standard numbers below are the usual way to meet them, but their current editions were **not verified** and must be checked against the FDA Recognized Consensus Standards database. For the device itself these are ISO 13485 (quality system), ISO 14971 (risk), IEC 60601-1, IEC 60601-1-2 (EMC), IEC 62304 (firmware), IEC 62366-1 (usability) and IEC 62133-2 plus UN 38.3 (Li-ion). For patient-contact and sterile parts they are ISO 10993-1, -5, -10 and -23, ISO 11135 (EtO) or ISO 11137 (radiation), ISO 11607 (packaging), ASTM F1980 (accelerated aging) and ASTM F1671 (barrier penetration). A cleared hysteroscopic shaver (K251923) confirms the device-side set and adds **IEC 60601-2-18** for endoscopic equipment ([K251923](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K251923)). Under EU MDR, Rules 6 and 9 mirror the Colombian rules, so CE marking would likely be Class IIa or IIb and require a notified body [EST].

## The competitive gap is containment, not cordlessness

The existing market falls into two groups. One is reusable, console-driven tube morcellators: Karl Storz Rotocut G1 and G2 (510(k) K061180), BOWA ERGO 300 at **12 and 15 mm** with a corded autoclavable micro-motor, Richard Wolf and Trokamed ([Storz listing](https://bioclinicalservices.com.au/karl-storz-endoskope/morcellator-systems); [BOWA brochure](https://www.duomed.com/sites/default/files/2023-06/BOWA-BRO-12873-MORCELLATION-20230119-EN.pdf); [OBG Management](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/Current_labeling_for_laparoscopic_power_morcellators_with_gynecologic_indications.pdf)). The other is a small number of single-use devices. The benchmark is **LiNA Xcise**: 15 mm, cordless, motor and battery in one device, "instant-use fully disposable." Its blade has three positions: exposed for coring, partly covered for peeling, and retracted into a guard ([LiNA brochure](https://pdf.medicalexpo.com/pdf/lina-medical/lina-xcise/113921-270071.html)). Olympus's PK Morcellator (510(k) 2016) added a pistol grip, better smoke evacuation and a peeling lip, and the FDA recommended pairing it with the PneumoLiner ([Drugwatch](https://www.drugwatch.com/news/2016/11/17/fda-approves-new-power-morcellator/)). The bipolar PlasmaSORD was labeled **not** for use with tissue bags ([OBG Management](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/Current_labeling_for_laparoscopic_power_morcellators_with_gynecologic_indications.pdf)). That is a strong hint that thermal cutting and thin bags do not mix, and that a mechanical blade is the right choice. Demand for exactly this category exists in the region: Panama's health ministry keeps a technical tender sheet for a "morcelador inalámbrico con obturador desechable" ([MINSA Panamá](https://ctni.minsa.gob.pa/Documentos/homvirt/2aa7ffef-327b-44cb-b531-4d02390a3462.doc)).

The containment evidence shows where a new device could actually help. The PneumoLiner takes morcellators with **15–18 mm shafts and 135–180 mm working length**. Setup involves widening the umbilical incision to about **2.0–2.5 cm**, a multilumen "boot," a preloaded bag, insufflating the bag with CO₂ and testing bag integrity at the end. Credentialing follows a "show, assist, do" model, and containment devices have no separate reimbursement ([Contemporary OB/GYN](https://www.contemporaryobgyn.net/view/containment-system-morcellation)). In a Munich series of 49 hysterectomies with the More-Cell-Safe bag, **6.1% of bags were not intact** afterwards. One had a 3 mm tear from the umbilical trocar, and two ruptured during forced extraction with myoma fragments still inside. Another **16.3%** had handling problems, such as twisting, piercing at insertion and repeated optic-trocar passes. Median bag-related time was 10 min (range 5–28) and grew with uterine size ([LMU study](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/55335/1/6701916.pdf)). Cohen et al. (AJOG 2016) found **dye leakage in 9.2%** of bags that looked intact, and their study stopped at interim because the protocol limit was 5% ([The Cancer Letter](https://cancerletter.com/the-cancer-letter/20160408_2/)). Pooled data show contained power morcellation taking **135.5 vs 93.3 min** for uncontained, with no difference in blood loss. The evidence behind that comparison is weak: observational studies, small samples and heterogeneous designs ([Marchand 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8711677)).

Taken together, these numbers say the main failure points are **trocar shear at the bag neck, forced extraction, and the port seal**, rather than the blade itself. Hidden seal leaks suggest that the neck and port interface matters as much as the film. That gives the design its brief [EST]: an integrated bag port so that no trocar pierces the bag; a blade guard and electronic torque limit that stop before the bag is snagged; extraction of the specimen inside the bag through a lower profile so the bag is not pulled hard; on-device smoke and visibility handling inside the inflated bag; and fewer setup steps than the PneumoLiner boot. A reusable core should also bring cost per case below that of fully disposable cordless devices. The same evidence argues for **routine dye-leak testing** in every early clinical case. It answers ACOG's open question and builds the SIM clinical file at the same time.

## Engineering the sealed core: torque through a wall, power without a port

### Cutting loads are unmeasured, so the first bench rig comes before any motor choice

The most important engineering fact in these notes is a gap: **no public source gives coring torque, cutting force or rpm for laparoscopic fibroid morcellation**. LiNA measured torque, rpm and g/min for its clearance but did not publish them ([K101458](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K101458)). The only published speed and rate data come from small hysteroscopic shavers: **300–1600 rpm** and about **10–20 g/min** in bovine heart for 4.5–4.8 mm tubes ([K251923](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K251923)). Fibroid stiffness measured by shear rheometry on 1 cm³ cubes is about **5.3 ± 0.9 kPa**, and collagen content drives stiffness ([WUSTL](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/loss-of-stiffness-in-collagen-rich-uterine-fibroids-after-digesti/)). That is a small-strain modulus, not a cutting toughness, so it cannot be used to size the motor. Arkenbout et al. found that "motor peeling" designs, in which the blade stays at the tissue surface and peels off a continuous strip, gave the highest morcellation rates. They also proposed a controlled-environment bag and a standard test protocol ([J Med Devices 2011](https://asmedc.silverchair.com/medicaldevices/article-pdf/5/2/027529/5928761/027529_1.pdf)).

On that basis, the working design numbers below are all **[EST] placeholders** to be replaced by bench data. For a 15 mm tube (r = 7.5 mm) and an assumed tangential cutting force of 10–30 N, blade torque is about **0.08–0.23 N·m**. At 0.2 N·m and 1000 rpm, shaft power is about **21 W**, or about **30–35 W electrical** at 60–70% drivetrain efficiency. Thirty minutes of continuous cutting would use about **17.5 Wh**, and a real intermittent duty cycle would use less. That points to a 2S–4S Li-ion pack of a few tens of Wh, kept well below the 100 Wh pack threshold that tightens transport rules (threshold from general knowledge, unverified). One sourced comparison is useful. The Maxon EC-max 22 is rated at **12 W and 10.8 mN·m nominal** ([maxon](https://www.maxongroup.com/maxon/view/product/283840)). By the estimate above that is undersized, so if the bench confirms loads near 0.2 N·m the motor class, and its price, goes up. The first build should therefore be a **bench rig**: a variable-speed motor with an in-line torque sensor driving 12 and 15 mm tubes into bovine heart and porcine uterus or myoma surrogates. It should record torque against rpm and feed rate, g/min and fragment scatter, and compare continuous rotation, oscillation and peeling. It costs little, it uses Nick's 3D printers for fixtures, and every later decision depends on its data.

### Moving torque across the seal

The user's central question, how to transmit motion across the seal, has three proven answers, and the magnetic one fits best. Synchronous coaxial magnetic couplings pass torque through a closed can with no shaft seal. KTR catalog data give **0.15 N·m at 38 mm outer diameter** and **1 N·m at 53 mm**, with NdFeB magnets rated to 150 °C ([KTR MINEX-S](https://www.morskatedrivetechnology.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/08/KTR-MINEX-S.pdf)). Two properties matter beyond sealing. **Metal cans cause eddy-current losses and heat**, while PEEK or ceramic walls avoid them. And when overloaded, a synchronous coupling **decouples**, interrupting transmission instead of slipping (same source). That gives a passive, hardware torque limit against snagging the bag. Disc (face) couplings tolerate up to 3° of misalignment and work through a flat barrier ([Magnetic Technologies](https://pdf.directindustry.com/pdf/magnetic-technologies/magnetic-couplings/26834-920478.html)). The second answer is a mechanical through-drape adapter, a dog clutch molded into a disposable insert, which is common in surgical robotics ([US 10,433,920](https://insight.rpxcorp.com/pat/US10433920B2)). It has higher torque density, but the integrity of the drape at the interface becomes a validation problem. The third is a flexible drive cable from a non-sterile drive, which Gynecare used in 1999 ([K993801](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K993801)). That works, but it defeats the purpose of a wireless device.

One design lever is worth testing early, and it follows from P = T·ω: **place the magnetic coupling on the higher-speed side of the gear reduction** [EST]. At about 21 W, the coupling has to carry 0.2 N·m at 1000 rpm but only about 0.04 N·m at 5000 rpm. That shrinks the face coupling from roughly 40 mm diameter toward a size that fits a pistol grip. The cost is a cheap single-stage reduction in the disposable cartridge (a molded POM or nylon planetary gear, acceptable for single use) and higher eddy losses at speed. The thin polymer wall (≤1 mm PEEK or PC, sterilized with the shell) avoids those losses. If the driven magnet half sits in the gamma- or EtO-sterilized disposable, NdFeB is fine. Only parts that see 134 °C steam need SmCo or derating ([KTR](https://www.morskatedrivetechnology.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/08/KTR-MINEX-S.pdf)). Pairing the coupling's mechanical decoupling with **motor-current torque limiting** in firmware gives two independent layers of protection against bag damage [EST]. Restart after a decoupling event must be validated so the motor stops, re-synchronizes and only then resumes.

### Keeping the core out of the autoclave

Inserting a non-sterile battery or electronics unit into a sterile housing is established, cleared practice. Stryker's SmartLife aseptic housings use a **sterile transfer shield** and two people. The sterile scrub opens the housing lid and fits the shield, the circulator drops in the non-sterile battery, the shield comes off, and the lid latches. The housing is **"designed and tested for 100 uses"**, and the IFU warns that a mis-latched lid "may open, exposing the non-sterile battery, which may result in patient injury" ([Stryker SmartLife IFU](https://bioclinicalservices.com.au/stryker/clinical/smartlife-aseptic-housings-and-transfer-shields-instructions-for-use-rev-ac-jan-2021)). The user's concept turns this around: the **sterile shell is single-use** and the non-sterile core is reused indefinitely, only wiped and disinfected, never sterilized. In that design the shell is a regulated sterile-barrier component. It needs barrier-integrity testing (ASTM F1671-type), latch and closure validation against the failure mode Stryker names, and usability validation of the two-person transfer under IEC 62366-1 [EST]. **No published data on breach rates for aseptic housings or sterile covers, and no FDA guidance specific to sterile covers, was found.** That gap must be filled, because it is the risk a reviewer will ask about first.

### Charging a unit that never opens

No source in this research addressed inductive charging or sealed contacts in surgical handpieces, so this subsection is **[EST]** throughout. The proven approach is a **swappable pack charged outside the field**, used by Stryker and by the Cyber Blade predicate with its AC charger ([US 8,400,108](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8400108); [K251923](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K251923)). The user's concept points to a better fit: because the core never needs sterilization, it can be **fully potted or ultrasonically welded with no openings** and charged **inductively** (Qi-class or a custom resonant link at the roughly 20–40 Wh scale) on a cradle in the sterile-processing or storage area. The same coil interface can carry NFC data, so the core reads the cartridge ID, use state and bag-interlock status through the shell wall. The alternatives are sealed pogo-pin or magnetic contacts on the core, simpler and more efficient but exposed to wipe-down chemicals, or a sealed swap pack. The validation questions are coil heating, charge time between cases, battery life over cycles and IEC 60601-1 internal-power-source requirements. A spare charged core per OR removes most of the charge-time risk.

## Patents: every single feature is taken, but the bag-cartridge integration looks open

### What already blocks or anticipates the obvious claims

*These are research notes, not a freedom-to-operate or patentability opinion. Legal status comes from Google Patents estimates, which Google itself calls "an assumption, not a legal conclusion."* The closest prior art to the cordless concept is **EP 2 593 025 B1** (Kebomed, now LiNA). Its claim 1 covers a disposable one-piece laparoscopic morcellator with a sharpened cutting cylinder, a sliding coaxial trocar that covers or exposes the edge, a one-hand housing, and **a motor and battery in the housing**. Priority is 2010, it is listed as active, and anticipated expiry is around **2031**. The family includes US 9,168,057 ([Google Patents EP2593025B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2593025B1)). A reusable core with a separate disposable cartridge is structurally different from a "one-piece disposable." Still, this family is prior art against any broad "battery-powered hand-held morcellator" claim. **Covidien US 11,871,952** (priority 2014, adjusted expiry ~2036) claims a disposable resecting unit with a cutting blade, a drive connector, and a **seal that keeps the fluid channel separate from the reusable drive** ([Google Patents US11871952](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11871952)). It belongs to a hysteroscopic, fluid-channel setting, but it covers the "disposable blade on reusable drive" pattern closely enough to be designed around carefully.

On the bag side, **Applied Medical's 2014–2015 families** form the densest active cluster. They include **US 10,463,352** (a bag with two openings joined by a flexible sidewall and a neck-sleeve extension, adjusted expiry ~2036) and **US 10,842,530** (a cut-resistant split-band shield rolled into a spiral with nesting corrugations that lock the lumen diameter, protecting bag and wound during in-bag cutting) ([Google Patents US10463352](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10463352); [uspto.report US10842530](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/10,842,530)). Several further members exist in the US, EP, AU and KR but were seen only by title. A bag design should **avoid the two-opening neck-sleeve layout and the corrugation-locked spiral shield**, or else plan to license them. For the sealed-core concept, **magnetic coupling through a sterile field barrier** is itself claimed, in US 12,508,093 (assignee not confirmed) ([USPTO PDF](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12508093)), and robotic sterile adaptors are heavily patented ([US 8,216,250](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8216250)). Aseptic battery transfer is old: Stryker's **US 8,400,108** is listed as **expired (adjusted 2026-03-24)** ([Google Patents US8400108](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8400108)). That lowers freedom-to-operate risk for the basic transfer-housing concept, but it also makes the concept free prior art against new claims. Later packaging patents such as US 12,220,264 may still be in force ([Google Patents US12220264](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12220264)). The examiner's natural combination is "LiNA battery morcellator + Stryker aseptic housing + robotic magnetic coupling = obvious." **The PneumoLiner's own patents were not identified**, and no patent claiming a morcellator pre-joined to a bag was found in a short search. That absence is not evidence that none exists.

### Candidate white space and how to claim it

The documented technical problem, hidden leaks and bag damage ([The Cancer Letter](https://cancerletter.com/the-cancer-letter/20160408_2/)), supports claims built on a specific problem and solution rather than on single features. The strongest candidates [EST, unverified white space] are as follows. First, a **single-use cutter cartridge supplied pre-joined and sealed to the bag port, with a mechanical or electronic interlock** so the blade cannot turn until the bag is deployed and sealed. Second, **bag-integrity sensing during morcellation**, such as pressure decay in the insufflated bag, a conductive or capacitive trace in the bag wall, or a cut-detection loop, linked to automatic motor stop, with the signal passing through the same shell interface that carries torque (NFC or inductive). Third, **torque or current signature detection** of the blade touching the bag film, as distinct from tissue. Fourth, a reusable core in a single-use shell whose motor-control logic is governed by **cartridge identity and interlock state** together. Fifth, bag and shield geometry that sits outside Applied Medical's claims. Each element alone is known, so the inventive step has to come from the combination and its measured technical effect. The bench rig is therefore also patent evidence. The recommended searches are CPC A61B17/32002, A61B17/00234, A61B2017/00287, A61B90/40, A61B2017/00734 and A61B90/98, plus a Patentscope query on "morcellat* AND bag AND (interlock OR sensor OR leak)."

### The SIC-to-PCT route and its traps

In Colombia an invention patent lasts **20 years** and requires novelty, inventive step and industrial application. A **utility model** lasts 10 years, protects "a new form, configuration or arrangement of elements" of a tool or mechanism that gives a technical advantage, needs no inventive-step examination, and runs on halved procedural times ([SIC guide](https://sedeelectronica.sic.gov.co/sites/default/files/otros/Gui%CC%81a%20-%20Co%CC%81mo%20tramitar%20una%20solicitud%20de%20patente%20en%20Colombia.pdf); [WIPO Lex, Decision 486 Arts. 81, 85](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/es/text/583519)). **Surgical, therapeutic and diagnostic methods are not patentable** ([SIC guide](https://sedeelectronica.sic.gov.co/sites/default/files/otros/Gui%CC%81a%20-%20Co%CC%81mo%20tramitar%20una%20solicitud%20de%20patente%20en%20Colombia.pdf)), so all claims should be written to devices and systems, not to how the surgeon morcellates. Novelty is absolute and worldwide. Decision 486 gives a **one-year grace period** for the inventor's own disclosures ([datojuridico summary](https://datojuridico.com/regimen-comun-de-las-patentes-en-la-comunidad-andina/)), but the EPO has no equivalent. A conference talk, poster, thesis defense or WhatsApp demo before filing can therefore destroy European rights. This is the single biggest IP risk in a project run by a student and a practicing surgeon. Official SIC fees (2025, Resolución 3676) are low: about **COP 112,500** to file an invention online and **COP 1,741,500** for examination. Discounts of 25–50% apply to individuals, MiPymes and universities, and 2026 amounts set by Resolución 77243 were not obtained ([Resolución 3676 de 2025](https://normograma.com/keralty/compilacion/docs/resolucion_superindustria_3676_2025.htm)). Colombia receives PCT national-phase entries (same source) and has **PPH** agreements with the USPTO, EPO, JPO and KIPO plus the Global PPH. A favorable US or EP result can cut a wait of two years or more at SIC to a few months ([Asuntos Legales](https://www.asuntoslegales.com.co/consultorio/procedimiento-acelerado-de-examen-de-patente-pph-en-colombia-3658652); [EPO](https://www.epo.org/en/news-events/news/epo-and-superintendence-industry-and-commerce-colombia-sic-make-their-pph-fast)). The sensible sequence is: a confidential Colombian invention filing with system claims; a **PCT filing within 12 months** claiming that priority; US and EP national phases at 30–31 months; and PPH back into Colombia. A utility model on the cartridge-to-port coupling geometry is a cheap fallback. Before filing, the two inventors should also settle **inventorship and ownership** between themselves, and with EIA if university resources or courses are used. EIA's IP policy was not researched.

The two readings of the cap lead to different businesses. **If the cap applies to the single-use kit** (cutter cartridge, shell, bag and port), an estimated cost of USD 30–120 leaves wide margin under USD 620. The real cost lies in one-time work rather than parts: ISO 11135 EtO validation at about **USD 10k–30k [EST]**, tooling, bag leak-validation testing and the clinical study. **If the cap applies to the reusable core**, it holds only with commodity motors and gearheads or high volume. A premium European drivetrain alone uses most of the budget, and the bench data may push the motor class up. Amortized over a Stryker-like 100+ uses [EST by analogy], the core costs per case are small either way. That is the commercial argument against fully disposable designs. Hard market prices are scarce. The only one found was an **expired** Alexis contained-extraction kit at USD 160.65, sold for teaching or research only ([Primis Medical](https://primismedical.com/products/applied-medical-gtb17-alexis-contained-extraction-system-17cm-6500ml)). A cost-conscious US laparoscopic hysterectomy using a disposable Morcellex totalled USD 1,705.60 in products ([MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/cost-conscious-choices-minimally-invasive-gynecologic-surgery)). A Turkish series found in-bag manual morcellation significantly cheaper per case than contained power morcellation ([BMC Surgery 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12893-023-02007-5)). The competitor on price is therefore not only other power devices but **a bag and a scalpel**. Methods such as ExCITE and the Alexis CES, which is cleared for **manual** extracorporeal morcellation ([MDedge ExCITE](https://mdedge.com/content/extracorporeal-c-incision-tissue-extraction-excite-technique); [Innolitics K211043](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/K211043)), set a low price ceiling. The product has to win on time saved and leak safety.

Colombian market size is unknown. A Popayán series found **38 of 138 hysterectomies (~28%) laparoscopic**, and its authors note that Colombia lacks national hysterectomy counts ([Rev Fac Med UNAL](https://revistas.unal.edu.co/index.php/revfacmed/article/download/73605/76053?inline=1)). A rough proxy of a few thousand morcellation-eligible procedures a year [EST] needs SISPRO and RIPS data by CUPS code before anyone builds a business plan on it. Local funding exists but on fixed calendars. Ruta N's **FutuMed 2026** offered up to **COP 6,000 M** pay-for-results for TRL 5–6 technologies, but it was limited to legal entities **at least one year old** in the Valle de Aburrá, and the call closed on 30 May 2026 ([Ruta N TDR](https://rutanmedellin.org/hubfs/futumed/TDR%20FutuMed%2026.pdf?hsLang=es)). Creating the company (S.A.S.) soon starts that one-year clock for the 2027 call. INVIMA's list of certified device establishments is the starting point for finding domestic manufacturers and sterilizers ([INVIMA](https://www.invima.gov.co/biblioteca/establecimientos_certificados_en_condiciones_sanitarias_de_dispositivos_m_c3_a9dicos_05-nov-2025_14pdf)).

## Recommended architecture and what to find out next

The notes support the following direction [EST synthesis]. The **reusable core** is a fully sealed, potted unit holding a BLDC motor with a first gear stage, a 2S–4S Li-ion pack with BMS, the MCU and motor driver, current-based torque sensing, and an inductive charge-and-NFC coil. It is wiped and disinfected, never sterilized, and has no exposed connectors. The **single-use sterile kit** contains a clamshell shell with a transfer-shield lid and a positive latch indicator, the driven half of a **face magnetic coupling** behind a ≤1 mm PEEK or PC wall, a cheap final reduction stage, a 15 mm blade tube with a retractable guard, an integrated port and seal, and a **bag pre-joined to the cartridge port with an interlock** and an NFC-readable ID. It is EtO-sterilized, or gamma-sterilized if the materials allow, and its shape stays clear of Applied Medical's two-opening and spiral-shield claims. For regulation, the plan is to target INVIMA Class IIa as a kit, with a written classification rationale and a contingency budget for IIb. The INVIMA file should be built to SIM special controls and ISO and IEC standards, and the Colombian clinical series should run as a registered study with dye-leak endpoints so it can support a US **SIM 510(k)**, or a De Novo if the umbilical or myomectomy indication departs from Claria's. Mechanical cutting is preferred over bipolar because of the PlasmaSORD precedent. This architecture puts the inventive weight, the regulatory differentiation and the clinical value in the same place: the **bag-cartridge interface**.

## Conclusion

The research changes the problem from "build a cordless morcellator" to **"build a better bag interface that happens to have a cordless motor."** Cordless and disposable are already solved and patented. The FDA's July 2026 SIM device type has just made the integrated morcellator-plus-containment system a recognized category, with a predicate to cite and a clinical-data requirement that suits a team with a surgeon at its center. That is the father's real advantage: not only clinical insight, but the ability to generate the dye-leak and time data that ACOG says is missing and that SIM requires. The sealed-core concept is technically sound and, by these estimates, the right economic choice. The commercial benchmark is a bag and a scalpel, not another console.

The project's critical path is therefore not the motor or the regulator. It is three cheap, near-term actions: **staying silent until a filing exists**, **measuring the cutting loads on a bench**, and **a professional search of the bag-cartridge white space**. If the search finds the interlock and integrity-sensing space open, the INVIMA Class IIa route gives a Medellín team a rare chance to put a contained device into clinical use quickly and turn that experience into US evidence. If the search finds it closed, the same bench data and prototypes still support a lower-cost reusable-core morcellator, though it would have to compete on price rather than patent protection.
